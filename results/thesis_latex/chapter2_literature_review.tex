\section{Research Papers}
\label{sec:research_papers}

For this research purpose we have referred to the following papers that provide us base and foundational knowledge in our research. They are:

\subsection{Random Forests: Ensemble Learning on High-Dimensional Tabular Telemetry}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Breiman~\cite{breiman2001random} introduced the Random Forest algorithm as an ensemble of unpruned decision trees built using bootstrap aggregation (bagging) and randomized feature subspace selection at every candidate split. The objective was to construct non-linear multi-class decision surfaces that significantly lower generalization variance without inducing excess bias. In network security engineering, this approach is applied by training an ensemble of axis-aligned trees over aggregated bidirectional flow measurements, evaluating split purity via the Gini impurity index.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The paper proved that random forests do not overfit as more trees are added, converging asymptotically to a limiting value of generalization error. On complex tabular telemetry, the ensemble achieves high multi-class precision and recall while maintaining computational efficiency during inference. Decision paths consist of simple threshold checks on scalar feature values, making inference fast on general-purpose processors without requiring floating-point matrix coprocessors.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The primary structural constraint of the algorithm is its strict adherence to a closed-world taxonomy. A decision tree ensemble maps incoming feature vectors exclusively into the leaf labels established during initial offline training. When presented with network traffic from an emergent zero-day attack family that was absent from the training split, the model has no mechanism to reject the sample or declare its uncertainty. It deterministically routes the unfamiliar flow to the nearest known historical class.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} Random Forest serves as our primary inline supervised classifier (Module M2). It provides the high line-rate classification accuracy required for known IoT network traffic, while establishing the baseline model whose catastrophic forgetting and misclassification patterns we examine when previously unseen attack flows arrive at the gateway.

\subsection{UNSW-NB15: Contemporary Multi-Class Network Intrusion Telemetry}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Moustafa and Slay~\cite{moustafa2015unsw} developed the UNSW-NB15 network benchmark to overcome the limitations of legacy datasets such as KDD Cup 1999 and NSL-KDD. Using the IXIA PerfectStorm tool in a synthetic testbed environment, the authors captured 100~GB of raw network traffic spanning modern normal activities and nine contemporary attack families (including Backdoors, DoS, Exploits, Generic attacks, and Reconnaissance). The raw packet traces were processed using the Bro (Zeek) network monitoring tool and custom scripts to extract 49 flow-level statistical, basic, and content features.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The study demonstrated that legacy datasets failed to represent modern network protocol dynamics, packet payload distributions, and complex attack signatures. Machine learning models trained on UNSW-NB15 revealed that contemporary attack flows share overlapping feature distributions with normal background traffic, making traditional deterministic signature matching insufficient and emphasizing the necessity of multi-dimensional statistical feature representation.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The dataset was gathered within a simulated commercial enterprise network environment and primarily captures standard server and client workstation interactions. It does not reflect the specialized lightweight protocol dynamics (such as MQTT, CoAP, and Modbus) or the constrained, bursty telemetry characteristic of operational IoT sensor and actuator networks.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} This paper informs our feature engineering and data preprocessing methodology (Module M1). In particular, it guides how transport-layer session metadata, connection duration, byte counts, and TCP state flags are aggregated into flow records suitable for lightweight edge inspection.

\subsection{Kitsune: Ensemble of Autoencoders for Online Network Anomaly Detection}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Mirsky et al.~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune} developed Kitsune, an unsupervised online intrusion detection framework designed for resource-constrained network appliances. The system uses an efficient feature extraction mechanism called the Damped Incremental Statistics Tree (D-Stat) to maintain running statistics of packet streams across different temporal decay windows. These features are routed through an ensemble of small, lightweight autoencoders (the KitNET anomaly detector), where each autoencoder monitors a distinct subset of correlated features and flags anomalies based on high reconstruction error.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} Kitsune demonstrated that accurate anomaly detection could execute online on low-cost hardware, including a single-board Raspberry Pi router, without dropping packets on gigabit connections. The autoencoder ensemble successfully detected novel network attacks, including ARP spoofing, video injection, and botnet propagation, without requiring labelled attack data during its initial setup.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} Kitsune is strictly a binary anomaly screener; it outputs an uncalibrated continuous anomaly score indicating that traffic deviates from the baseline, but cannot classify the threat into specific attack families or provide actionable intelligence for targeted mitigation. Furthermore, it requires periodic baseline updating on normal traffic, rendering it vulnerable to adversarial poisoning if an attack persists during its background training window.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} Kitsune provides proof that multi-model, streaming anomaly detection is feasible on edge gateway hardware. This directly motivates our decoupled Stage 1 architecture, where an unsupervised screener runs alongside a supervised classifier to identify unfamiliar traffic distributions without burdening the main classification engine.

\subsection{Isolation Forest: Fast Tree-Based Unsupervised Outlier Identification}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2008isolation} formulated the Isolation Forest algorithm for unsupervised anomaly detection. Instead of constructing statistical profiles of normal data density or calculating expensive pairwise distances between points, the method explicitly isolates anomalies. Exploiting the property that anomalous points are few and structurally distinct in feature space, the algorithm builds an ensemble of completely randomized binary trees (iTrees), where each node splits a randomly selected attribute at a uniformly chosen value between its minimum and maximum. Anomalies are isolated near the root, resulting in shorter average path lengths.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The algorithm achieves a low time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(B \cdot \psi \log \psi)$, where $B$ is the tree count and $\psi$ is a small subsample size (typically 256), and requires minimal memory. The authors showed that Isolation Forest outperforms distance-based methods like Local Outlier Factor (LOF) and One-Class SVMs on high-dimensional benchmarks, maintaining high detection fidelity even in datasets containing multiple irrelevant attributes.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} While effective at flagging global spatial outliers, Isolation Forest provides only an uncalibrated binary score (inlier versus outlier). When applied to subtle or stealthy network intrusions whose statistical profiles closely resemble benign traffic, its detection recall is moderate. It cannot serve as a standalone intrusion prevention system because it cannot distinguish between different attack families.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} Isolation Forest is adopted directly as our Stage 1 anomaly screener (Module M3). Fitted strictly on known baseline training traffic with an untuned threshold, it operates as an autonomous, low-overhead trigger that detects unfamiliar zero-day flows and routes them into a quarantine queue for analyst verification.

\subsection{TON\_IoT: Modern Cyber-Physical and Telemetry Benchmark}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Alsaedi et al.~\cite{alshammari2020toniot} introduced the TON\_IoT telemetry and network dataset, collected from an Industry 4.0 physical-virtual testbed at the Australian Defence Force Academy. The testbed integrated real IoT field devices, physical SCADA systems, industrial sensors, virtual machines, and cloud layers under concurrent operation. The authors executed nine distinct contemporary attack vectors: Backdoors, Denial-of-Service, Distributed Denial-of-Service, Injection attacks, Ransomware, Port Scanning, Password cracking, Man-in-the-Middle, and Cross-Site Scripting.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The study established that the TON\_IoT dataset exhibits wide variation in attack volume, reflecting operational conditions where low-rate stealthy attacks (such as MITM sessions comprising only a few hundred records) coexist with aggressive volumetric floods (such as Backdoors and Ransomware comprising tens of thousands of flows). The authors demonstrated that conventional machine learning models show significant performance variations across these attack profiles.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The dataset contains duplicate flow entries resulting from multiple collection sensors monitoring identical network interfaces. Furthermore, raw IP addresses and default target columns can lead to artificial memorization if not properly cleaned and preprocessed before model training.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} TON\_IoT serves as our primary experimental testbed. We use its heterogeneous attack profiles to conduct leave-one-attack-out evaluations across low-volume stealthy attacks (MITM) and high-volume attacks (Ransomware, XSS, and Backdoors), evaluating model adaptation across realistic operational volume variations.

\subsection{Overcoming Catastrophic Forgetting: Parameter Regularization and EWC}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Kirkpatrick et al.~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} addressed the problem of catastrophic forgetting in artificial neural networks, where learning a new task overwrites the network weights required for previous tasks. Drawing inspiration from synaptic consolidation in mammalian brains, the authors proposed Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC). EWC computes the diagonal elements of the Fisher Information Matrix over earlier task data to identify parameter importance, adding a quadratic regularization penalty to the loss function that slows down learning on weights critical to older tasks.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} EWC successfully enabled deep networks to learn sequences of separate tasks (such as playing multiple Atari video games sequentially) without forgetting earlier ones. The authors demonstrated that the network retains earlier capabilities by moving along low-loss manifolds in parameter space, achieving a balance between stability and plasticity.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} EWC relies fundamentally on calculating loss gradients and second-order Fisher information matrices across continuous, differentiable parameter spaces. It is mathematically incompatible with non-parametric, rule-based, or decision tree ensembles like Random Forests, which rely on greedy coordinate-aligned splits rather than gradient descent. Furthermore, the memory required to store parameter importance scales with model size.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} This paper provides the theoretical basis for our understanding of the stability--plasticity dilemma. Recognizing that gradient-based parameter regularization cannot be used with decision tree ensembles on edge gateways led us directly to explore data-level experience replay.

\subsection{Synaptic Intelligence: Online Continual Learning via Path Integral Metrics}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Zenke, Poole, and Ganguli~\cite{zenke2017continual} proposed Synaptic Intelligence (SI) as an alternative continual learning mechanism to resolve catastrophic forgetting. Rather than calculating parameter importance offline at task boundaries using Fisher information, SI calculates an online path integral of the gradient trajectory during standard training, measuring each individual synapse's contribution to changes in the global loss function. Important parameters are then constrained using a quadratic regularization penalty during subsequent task optimization.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The authors demonstrated that SI achieves retention on sequential image classification benchmarks comparable to or better than EWC, while computing parameter importance online during training without a separate evaluation pass. This verified that tracking parameter trajectories enables continual adaptation in deep neural architectures.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} Similar to EWC, Synaptic Intelligence is tied strictly to gradient-based continuous optimization. It cannot be applied to decision trees, where parameter updates are discrete and non-differentiable. Additionally, tracking trajectory metrics for every weight increases runtime memory overhead during training.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} The conceptual foundation of Synaptic Intelligence reinforces the importance of tracking class retention during model updates. This guided our development of the Plasticity--Stability Index (PSI) to measure the balance between acquiring new attack classes and retaining older ones.

\subsection{iCaRL: Incremental Classifier and Representation Learning via Exemplars}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Rebuffi et al.~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl} introduced iCaRL, an exemplar-based continual learning framework for class-incremental learning in computer vision. When exposed to a stream of new classes, iCaRL stores a small, fixed-size memory buffer of exemplar samples from earlier classes. Classification is performed using a nearest-mean-of-exemplars rule in feature space, and exemplar sets are selected using herding to approximate the true class mean. During model updates, the buffered exemplars are combined with new class data using distillation loss to prevent representational drift.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The study proved that retaining a small, well-chosen subset of past training examples preserves class decision boundaries across long sequences of new tasks, outperforming regularization-only methods. The authors showed that data-level replay prevents decision boundaries from collapsing toward newly added classes.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} iCaRL was engineered around deep convolutional feature extractors and relies on a static, fixed total memory budget divided evenly among all seen classes ($m = B/K$). When incoming class volumes vary significantly, this static allocation causes class imbalance in the replay buffer.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} iCaRL demonstrates that exemplar replay is a practical way to maintain past knowledge during incremental learning. This directly supports our choice of experience replay as the adaptation mechanism for tree ensembles on edge gateways.

\subsection{Tiny Episodic Memories: Sample-Efficient Continual Learning via Buffer Replay}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Chaudhry et al.~\cite{chaudhry2019tiny} investigated the empirical performance of tiny episodic memory buffers in continual learning. Comparing complex parameter-regularization techniques against rehearsal replay on sequential benchmarks (including Split-CIFAR and MiniImageNet), the authors evaluated how many past exemplars a model actually needs to prevent catastrophic forgetting, testing static buffers alongside random reservoir sampling based on Vitter's Algorithm~R~\cite{vitter1985random}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The paper delivered a key empirical finding: extremely small replay buffers (as few as 10 to 50 samples per class) matched or outperformed complex parameter regularization algorithms like EWC and GEM, while requiring substantially lower computational overhead. Storing a handful of past samples acts as a strong geometric anchor during retraining, preventing decision boundary collapse with minimal memory cost.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The authors evaluated memory sizes only under balanced benchmark conditions where all tasks had equal numbers of samples. They did not investigate the volumetric imbalance that occurs in network security, where a new attack batch can contain ten to twenty times more flows than the historical buffer.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} This paper directly motivates our Phase 7.1 sensitivity experiments, where we systematically reduce historical buffer capacity down to 25 samples per class to find the minimum memory needed to prevent forgetting on an edge gateway.

\subsection{Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM): Constrained Optimization in Continual Learning}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Lopez-Paz and Ranzato~\cite{lopezpaz2017gem} developed Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) to balance backward and forward transfer in lifelong learning. GEM maintains an episodic memory buffer for each past task. When optimizing the model on a new task, the loss gradients are projected using quadratic programming to ensure that the updated weights do not increase the loss on any stored past exemplars, turning replay into an inequality-constrained optimization problem.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} GEM demonstrated strong backward transfer (BWT), proving that constrained replay updates not only prevent catastrophic forgetting but can sometimes improve performance on earlier tasks. The authors formalized the mathematical definitions of backward transfer and average forgetting that are now standard across continual learning research.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} GEM requires solving a dual quadratic programming problem over all stored exemplars at every gradient step. This makes it computationally heavy and impractical for resource-constrained edge gateways. Furthermore, its mathematical formulation requires continuous gradient vectors, making it incompatible with decision tree ensembles.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} We adopt the formal mathematical definitions of Backward Transfer ($\text{BWT} = -\mathcal{F}_{\text{avg}}$) and per-class forgetting introduced in GEM, using them to evaluate model stability in Module M7.

\subsection{Replay or Regret: Benchmarking Continual Learning in Network Intrusion Detection}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Cormier, Labio, and Garc{\'i}a~\cite{replay_or_regret2023} conducted an empirical study comparing continual learning methods on network intrusion detection datasets. The authors evaluated parameter regularization (EWC), knowledge distillation (LwF), and rehearsal-based experience replay across sequential multi-class attack streams, tracking model performance as new attack classes were introduced one after another.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The study found that rehearsal-based experience replay consistently outperformed parameter regularization and distillation methods in retaining multi-class detection accuracy. Replay maintained high detection rates on earlier attack classes, whereas regularization-only approaches suffered substantial accuracy drops as task sequences grew longer.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The authors used a fixed static buffer quota (e.g., a constant 50 or 100 samples per class) throughout their experiments, and did not examine what happens when incoming novel attacks are volumetric. Furthermore, they did not investigate how class imbalance affects CART Gini impurity calculations during decision tree retraining.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} This paper represents the closest prior work to our investigation. Its findings confirm that replay is the most effective continual learning strategy for intrusion detection. Crucially, its reliance on fixed buffer quotas defines the starting point for our work on Dynamic Replay.

\subsection{Zero-Day Attack Detection in the Internet of Things: Contemporary Challenges}

\noindent\textbf{Objective and methodology:} Shiltagh and Al-Jamali~\cite{shiltagh2025zeroday} surveyed recent techniques for detecting zero-day attacks in IoT networks. The authors evaluated supervised classifiers, deep learning architectures, and unsupervised anomaly detectors, analyzing the operational challenges of deploying these models in real-world environments.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Results and findings:} The survey highlighted the disconnect between anomaly detection and model adaptation in published literature. While unsupervised detectors can flag unusual traffic, they cannot identify the specific attack type. Meanwhile, supervised classifiers perform well on familiar attacks but fail completely when confronted with novel intrusions, misclassifying unseen attacks into existing categories. The authors emphasized the need for adaptive systems that can turn anomaly detections into updated multi-class decision boundaries on edge devices.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Limitations and scope:} The survey focuses on high-level architectural concepts and taxonomy without providing a concrete implementation, mathematical framework, or empirical evaluation of on-device continual retraining.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Application in our research:} This paper confirms the real-world need for our two-stage architecture, which bridges the gap between unsupervised anomaly screening (Stage 1) and low-latency on-device model adaptation (Stage 2).

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

The twelve research papers reviewed in this chapter provide the foundational context, algorithmic tools, and benchmark comparisons that frame our study:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Supervised Classification and Tree Ensembles:} Breiman~\cite{breiman2001random} established Random Forests as fast, robust classifiers for tabular data, while Moustafa and Slay~\cite{moustafa2015unsw} and Alsaedi et al.~\cite{alshammari2020toniot} provided realistic intrusion benchmarks. Together, they demonstrate that while tree ensembles achieve high accuracy on known traffic, they operate under a closed-world assumption that fails when previously unseen attacks arrive.
    \item \textbf{Unsupervised Anomaly Screening:} Mirsky et al.~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune} and Liu et al.~\cite{liu2008isolation} showed that unsupervised anomaly detectors can isolate unfamiliar traffic patterns without requiring attack labels during training. However, because these methods yield only binary outlier scores, they are best suited as triggers for adaptation rather than standalone multi-class classifiers, a gap highlighted by Shiltagh and Al-Jamali~\cite{shiltagh2025zeroday}.
    \item \textbf{Continual Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting:} Kirkpatrick et al.~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} and Zenke et al.~\cite{zenke2017continual} demonstrated that sequential model updates cause catastrophic forgetting, but their regularization techniques require differentiable neural networks. Rebuffi et al.~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}, Chaudhry et al.~\cite{chaudhry2019tiny}, and Lopez-Paz and Ranzato~\cite{lopezpaz2017gem} proved that experience replay works at the data level, making it compatible with non-parametric decision tree ensembles.
    \item \textbf{The Replay Gap in Intrusion Detection:} While Cormier et al.~\cite{replay_or_regret2023} confirmed that experience replay is the most effective continual learning strategy for intrusion detection, existing approaches rely on fixed static buffer quotas. When an incoming attack is volumetric, this fixed allocation breaks down, causing severe catastrophic forgetting on earlier classes.
\end{enumerate}
