\section{Background of the Study}
\label{sec:background_study}

The growth of the Internet of Things (IoT) has brought connected computing into everyday utility and industrial networks, from building management and automated manufacturing to smart electricity grids and medical monitors. Most field devices in these setups are small sensors and actuators built around basic microcontrollers. They are designed to be cheap and consume little power, which means they usually have very little memory and limited processing capacity. 

Because these endpoints cannot run standard antivirus tools, endpoint agents, or virtual machines, securing the network cannot happen on the devices themselves. Instead, defense has to be handled at the network edge. A security system running on a local router or gateway must monitor traffic passing through, catch malicious behavior early, and stop an attacker from moving deeper into the network.

\section{Servers and Their Operational Role}
\label{sec:gateways_operational_role}

Edge gateways sit between local sensor networks and wider corporate or cloud networks. They translate communication protocols, route packets, collect sensor readings, and filter traffic passing across the boundary.

\section{Hardware and Firmware Errors in Servers}
\label{sec:hardware_firmware_constraints}

Running machine learning models on edge devices means working within practical hardware limits:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Limited Memory (RAM):} Edge gateways usually have modest memory, typically between 1 GB and 4 GB. This memory has to be shared across the operating system, packet buffers, routing tables, and local services. Security programs must keep their memory usage small to prevent system crashes.
    \item \textbf{Flash Storage Wear:} Gateways use flash memory (eMMC or NAND chips), which can only be written to a certain number of times before wearing out. Continuously writing large traffic logs to disk for retraining can quickly damage the hardware.
    \item \textbf{CPU and Thermal Limits:} Field devices often sit in sealed enclosures without fans. Running heavy training tasks for long periods causes the processor to heat up and throttle, leading to dropped packets.
    \item \textbf{Minimal Software Environment:} Gateway firmware builds are kept simple and lightweight. They lack heavy software packages and specialized graphics drivers, so any security program must run directly on standard processors.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Operational Impact and Research Motivation}
\label{sec:operational_impact_motivation}

Machine learning models, especially tree-based ensembles like Random Forest, are widely used for intrusion detection because they make decisions quickly, run efficiently on ordinary processors, and handle complex tabular network data well. On static benchmark datasets, these models frequently reach classification accuracy above 98 percent.

However, these models rely on a closed-world assumption: they assume that real-world traffic will always belong to the classes they were trained on. In practice, attackers constantly change their methods, introduce new tools, and alter traffic patterns. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fp}, a static classifier cannot recognize an attack it has never seen before. Instead, it forces the flow into whichever known category looks most similar.

This causes two major operational problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Silent Evasion:} An unfamiliar stealthy attack may be labeled as normal traffic, allowing the intrusion to slip past undetected.
    \item \textbf{Misleading Alerts:} The attack might be misclassified into the wrong threat category, causing responders to address the wrong issue and creating alarm fatigue.
\end{itemize}

    \label{fig:closed_world_failure}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:closed_world_failure} illustrates this on the TON\_IoT dataset. While the baseline Random Forest achieved an average F1 score of 0.989 on familiar classes, its F1 score on unseen Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) traffic dropped to exactly 0.000. All of the unseen flows were misclassified: 29.3 percent were passed as normal traffic, and 39.9 percent were mislabeled as Denial-of-Service. To solve this, a gateway needs an adaptive approach (Figure~\ref{fig:ip}) that can detect unfamiliar activity and update its model directly on the device.

\section{Challenges in Diagnosing Server Errors}
\label{sec:challenges_diagnosing_attacks}

Building an adaptive detector on edge hardware involves several practical challenges:

\subsection{There Are Multiple Factors That May Result in Similar Symptoms}
\label{subsec:multiple_factors_symptoms}
Different network events often produce very similar numbers in flow summaries. A sudden spike in connection attempts and packet counts could be a denial-of-service attack, an exfiltration attempt by ransomware, port reconnaissance, or simply a group of industrial sensors reconnecting after a brief network drop. Telling malicious activity apart from normal network surges requires careful decision boundaries.

\subsection{One Issue May Cause Multiple Messages}
\label{subsec:one_issue_multiple_messages}
A single attack campaign often generates different types of traffic across several stages. Initial reconnaissance produces brief port probes, the actual exploit generates application payloads, command-and-control communication creates periodic beacons, and lateral movement produces authentication traffic. Grouping these separate observations into a single coherent incident is a difficult task for a local detector.

\subsection{Different Terminology and Structure by Vendors}
\label{subsec:different_terminology_structure}
IoT networks connect equipment from many different manufacturers using a variety of protocols, such as MQTT, CoAP, Modbus, and standard TCP/IP. Logged attributes often have missing values, different field names, or varying connection states. An edge detector must handle these structural variations without losing accuracy.

\subsection{Condition Description and Recommendations Have Different Purpose}
\label{subsec:condition_description_recommendations}
Edge security tools have two different jobs. They must provide detailed, readable information for human security staff to review, but they also need to make immediate filtering decisions and update models within fractions of a second to block ongoing attacks.

\section{Existing Troubleshooting Practices}
\label{sec:existing_troubleshooting_practices}

Common methods used to maintain edge network detectors today have clear weaknesses:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signature Rule Updates:} Rule-based engines depend on vendors to create and distribute signatures after a threat is analyzed. This process often takes days or weeks, fails against modified attacks, and uses significant processor capacity when scanning traffic against long rule lists.
    \item \textbf{Cloud Batch Retraining:} Gateways send traffic logs to cloud servers, which retrain models from scratch and send updated weights back to the field. This uses substantial internet bandwidth, leaves gateways unprotected during the retraining interval, and can violate data privacy rules for sensitive environments.
    \item \textbf{Coarse Rate Limiting:} When unexplained traffic spikes occur, operators often resort to broad connection limits or temporary port shutdowns. These crude measures frequently block legitimate traffic, disrupting industrial operations and plant monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:research_gap}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{results/figures/chapter1/fig1_2_update_dilemma.pdf}
    \caption{The model update dilemma on TON\_IoT: (a) New-attack F1 score achieved by different update strategies; (b) Known-class retention accuracy; (c) Historical sample storage requirements (log scale); and (d) Wall-clock retraining latency. Static Full Retraining sacrifices rare-attack plasticity and violates edge memory constraints, while New-Data-Only adaptation induces catastrophic forgetting.}
    \label{fig:update_dilemma}
\end{figure}

When a gateway receives a verified batch of new attack samples, existing update approaches run into a three-way dilemma, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:update_dilemma}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Forgetting:} Retraining only on the new attack overwrites existing decision boundaries. The model learns the new threat but completely forgets earlier classes, dropping known-class accuracy to zero.
    \item \textbf{Resource Limits and Dilution in Full Retraining:} Retraining on all historical traffic plus the new data avoids forgetting, but it requires storing over 127,000 flow records and takes considerable computation time. Furthermore, if the new attack is rare (such as our MITM batch of 207 flows), it makes up only a tiny fraction of the data. Decision trees focus on the dominant classes, and the model fails to learn the new attack properly, reaching an F1 score of only 0.648.
    \item \textbf{Volumetric Imbalance in Static Replay:} Storing a small, fixed buffer of past samples (such as 100 flows per class) works well for small attacks, reaching an F1 of 0.977. However, fixed buffers fail when a large attack arrives, such as ransomware or backdoor floods involving thousands of flows.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{results/figures/chapter1/fig1_3_buffer_vs_volume.pdf}
    \caption{Impact of incoming novel attack volume on maximum known-class forgetting. Fixed replay buffers suffer sharp forgetting spikes when volumetric attacks overwhelm the stored exemplars, whereas the proposed Dynamic Replay allocation keeps forgetting bounded.}
    \label{fig:buffer_vs_volume}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:buffer_vs_volume}, when new attack flows heavily outnumber the historical buffer, the stored past samples become a small minority. The decision trees split mainly to separate the new attack, causing forgetting on earlier classes to rise sharply to nearly 0.40. 

Current literature lacks an adaptive replay method that adjusts buffer sizes to match incoming attack volumes while staying within a fixed, modest memory budget on edge hardware.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}

The central question addressed in this research is:

How can an intrusion detection system running on an edge IoT gateway detect unfamiliar zero-day attacks without prior labels, and adapt its model on the device to achieve high accuracy on the new attack while preventing significant forgetting of previously learned classes, under both small and large attack volumes, while using less than 1 MB of memory and completing updates within 0.1 seconds?

\section{Main Concepts and Tools}
\label{sec:main_concepts_tools}

\subsection{Knowledge and Concepts}
\label{subsec:knowledge_concepts}
Network traffic is represented as communication sessions called flows. A flow groups packets that share the same five characteristics: source IP address, destination IP address, source port, destination port, and protocol. From these sessions, features are extracted to measure traffic volume (byte and packet totals), timing behavior (flow duration and inter-arrival intervals), and protocol flags.

\subsection{Classes and Relationships}
\label{subsec:classes_relationships}
Traffic belongs to a set of known categories, including normal operational traffic and recognized attack types. In feature space, each class forms a distinct distribution. When an unfamiliar attack appears, it creates a new pattern. The goal of continual learning is to identify this new pattern without distorting the boundaries established for existing classes.

\subsection{Ontology}
\label{subsec:ontology_concepts}
Ontologies provide a structured vocabulary for describing security threats. By modeling attack behaviors in a standardized way, detected anomalies can be linked directly to established security frameworks, such as MITRE ATT\&CK, making alerts clearer for human review.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph}
\label{subsec:knowledge_graphs}
Knowledge graphs connect security concepts to physical devices and network events. On a gateway, a local knowledge graph helps trace whether an anomaly detected on one sensor is related to activity on neighboring devices.

\subsection{RDF, OWL and Storage Formats}
\label{subsec:rdf_owl_storage}
Semantic descriptions are stored using standard formats like the Resource Description Framework (RDF) and Web Ontology Language (OWL). On edge hardware, compact representations are used to keep storage requirements small.

\subsection{Ontological Reasoning}
\label{subsec:ontological_reasoning}
The system uses both statistical learning and rule-based logic. The machine learning models evaluate high-dimensional traffic data, while rule checks ensure that alerts align with defined network security policies.

\subsection{Construction and Validation Tools}
\label{subsec:construction_validation_tools}
The experimental framework is written in Python, using Scikit-Learn for tree models, and NumPy and Pandas for data processing. Automated hashing tests verify that data does not leak between training and test sets.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:research_objectives}

This work addresses four specific research objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Objective 1:} Measure the failure of static Random Forest models on unfamiliar attacks using a leave-one-out setup on the TON\_IoT dataset, and test whether an untuned Isolation Forest can reliably flag novel traffic to trigger adaptation.
    \item \textbf{Objective 2:} Test whether a balanced replay buffer of 100 samples per class can match or outperform full retraining on rare attacks, while cutting storage and retraining time.
    \item \textbf{Objective 3:} Test buffer sizes from 25 to 500 samples per class to find the point where memory savings and retention balance best.
    \item \textbf{Objective 4:} Measure how fixed replay buffers behave under large attacks, and design a volume-aware dynamic replay rule that limits forgetting, keeps memory usage under 1 MB, and updates within 0.1 seconds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

Four research questions guide this investigation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Question 1:} How badly does a static classifier fail on an unfamiliar attack, and can an Isolation Forest reliably flag novel flows without prior labels?
    \item \textbf{Question 2:} Why does full retraining perform poorly on small, rare attacks, and how does a balanced replay buffer solve this problem?
    \item \textbf{Question 3:} What is the practical minimum number of samples needed per class to prevent forgetting, and where do returns start to diminish?
    \item \textbf{Question 4:} Why do fixed buffers fail when novel attacks are large, and how well does volume-aware replay resolve this issue across different attack types?
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Approach}
\label{sec:evaluation_approach}

The evaluation follows a clear experimental methodology:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Realistic Dataset:} Experiments are run on the TON\_IoT network dataset, which includes both modern IoT devices and industrial traffic.
    \item \textbf{Data Cleaning:} IP addresses are removed to prevent devices from simply memorizing specific addresses, and duplicate rows are removed, leaving 183,592 clean flow records.
    \item \textbf{Leave-One-Attack-Out Testing:} Four different attacks with varying volumes are tested as unseen zero-days: MITM (rare, 207 flows), Ransomware (medium, 2,853 flows), XSS (large, 2,903 flows), and Backdoor (large, 3,742 flows).
    \item \textbf{Separation of Training and Testing:} Data transformations and encoders are fitted only on the training portion, and hash checks confirm negligible overlap with the test set.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Metrics:} Performance is tracked using per-class F1 scores, overall accuracy, average forgetting, maximum forgetting, memory reduction, and update time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope_study}

The scope of this research is defined by the following boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Target Hardware:} The framework is designed for IoT edge gateways and local routers, not tiny battery-powered sensors or large cloud data centers.
    \item \textbf{Model Selection:} We focus on Random Forest and Isolation Forest ensembles, which run efficiently on gateway processors without requiring GPUs.
    \item \textbf{Traffic Features:} We analyze flow-level summary features, such as packet counts and duration, rather than deep inspection of encrypted packet payloads.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitation}
\label{sec:limitations_study}

Several practical limitations apply to this work:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Attack Updates:} Experiments evaluate adding one new attack at a time to an existing model. Handling a continuous sequence of many attacks over time remains an area for future study.
    \item \textbf{Label Verification:} The framework assumes that once anomalous flows are flagged, an analyst or external threat feed confirms the labels before retraining begins.
    \item \textbf{Test Hardware:} Timing and memory figures were measured on multi-core desktop processors, serving as a comparative baseline for edge evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions of the Study}
\label{sec:contributions_study}

This thesis makes six main contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Demonstrating Closed-World Failure:} We show that a static classifier with over 99 percent accuracy on known traffic fails completely on an unseen attack, misclassifying all novel flows and passing nearly 30 percent as normal traffic.
    \item \textbf{Evaluating Anomaly Screening:} We demonstrate that an untuned Isolation Forest flags unseen attack traffic at nearly seven times the rate of familiar attacks, providing a practical trigger for model updates.
    \item \textbf{Explaining Replay Behavior:} We show why full retraining struggles on rare attacks due to class imbalance, and demonstrate that a balanced replay buffer achieves an F1 score of 0.977 on rare threats while using 99 percent less storage.
    \item \textbf{Identifying Minimum Buffer Size:} Through sensitivity testing, we show that 100 samples per class provides an effective balance between memory savings and forgetting prevention.
    \item \textbf{Developing Dynamic Replay:} We identify that static buffers fail under large attacks, with the smallest known class losing the most accuracy. We introduce a volume-aware replay rule that cuts maximum forgetting by 20 to 36 percent while keeping memory under 1 MB and retraining in under 0.09 seconds.
    \item \textbf{Providing a Verified Pipeline:} We provide a clean, reproducible evaluation pipeline with verified data separation, establishing a reliable baseline for edge continual learning.
\end{enumerate}
